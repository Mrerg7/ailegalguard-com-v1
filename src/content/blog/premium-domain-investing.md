Domain names sit in an odd place in an investor's mental map. They are not securities, they do not pay dividends, they trade on opaque private markets, and the "exchange" is a negotiation between two people. And yet the top tier of the market has compounded quietly for two decades while plenty of conventional assets went sideways.

Here is an honest look at how the asset class actually behaves.

## What you are buying

A premium domain is closer to **land than to stock**:

- **Supply is fixed and non-renewable.** There is one `law.com`. Every year, more people want short, meaningful `.com` names and the registry cannot print more of them.
- **Carrying cost is small but permanent.** Registration renewals run from a few dollars to low hundreds per year. A portfolio is a liability line, not a free option.
- **Value is derived, not intrinsic.** A domain is worth what the next buyer will pay for the utility it confers. If the category dies, so does the price.

That last point is the one people underestimate. Domains are not stores of value in the way bullion is; they are **options on future demand for a word**.

## Why `.com` keeps winning

Every few years a new TLD is announced as the `.com killer`. The pattern has been remarkably consistent:

1. A wave of registrations by speculators and early adopters.
2. Enthusiastic resale listings at aspirational prices.
3. Gradual realisation that **end users still type `.com` by default**, and that brands treat a non-`.com` as a compromise to be fixed later.

The reason is not technical, it is behavioural. `.com` is what people say out loud, what gets printed on signage, and what survives being misheard. A name that requires explaining ("no, dot-A-I, not dot-A") has a permanent tax attached.

For an investor, that means `.com` inventory ages differently from everything else in the namespace: the good ones tend to get *more* scarce, not less.

## The liquidity problem

This is the risk that deserves the most honesty.

**Domain markets are illiquid.** There is no order book, no mark-to-market, and no guarantee any given name will sell at any given price in any given decade. Typical experiences:

- A strong name sells in weeks to the right outreach list.
- A good name sells in six to eighteen months to the natural buyer.
- A plausible name sits in a portfolio for years and eventually expires.

The market is also **bifurcated**. Liquid, expensive names (short dictionary words, exact category terms) attract real demand. Everything long-tail — clever combinations, hyphenated names, non-standard TLDs — often has a listed price and no bid.

## What separates a durable holding from dead capital

Experienced portfolio holders converge on the same filters:

- **Buy for a buyer, not for a story.** Every name should have a short, obvious list of who would want it — a law firm, a compliance platform, a fund — and why they would pay.
- **Prefer `.com`, short, pronounceable.** These three filters eliminate most of the market immediately, which is the point.
- **Avoid live trademark exposure.** A name that collides with an active brand in a relevant class is not a cheap option; it is a liability with a renewal date.
- **Budget for renewals honestly.** Carrying costs are small individually and meaningful in aggregate. Names you would not renew are names you should not have bought.
- **Be willing to hold, or willing to sell.** The middle path — holding and never marketing — is where returns go to die.

## Returns: the honest version

There is no index you can buy, and no published distribution of returns, because most transactions are private and most portfolios are not marked.

What can be said with confidence:

- **The top of the market has done well.** Short, meaningful `.com` names sold in the early 2000s have appreciated dramatically, and widely reported eight-figure sales show the ceiling keeps rising.
- **The middle and long tail have not.** For every name that doubled, there is a portfolio of hyphenated combinations that renewed for a decade and expired unsold.
- **The return is concentrated.** This looks less like a bond ladder and more like venture or art: most of the value sits in a small number of positions, and the outcome depends heavily on doing the acquisition right.

Treat it accordingly — size positions as speculative, expect a multi-year hold, and never put money here you cannot leave untouched through a full cycle.

## How it fits alongside everything else

Domains are a **speculative, illiquid, concentrated** exposure with a low carrying cost and a long tail of upside. For most people that is a small satellite position, not a core one.

The strongest reason to hold them is not financial engineering. It is the simplest one: **someone is going to own the good names.** If your business or your industry depends on a particular word being available, owning it outright converts a recurring, permanent cost into a one-time purchase.

That is an operating decision as much as an investment one — and for the natural buyer of a category-defining name, it is usually the most rational transaction available.
