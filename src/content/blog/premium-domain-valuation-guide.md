There is no published price list for domain names. Unlike property, there is no multiple-listing service, no public deed registry, and no per-square-metre rate you can look up. Most transactions are private, so the number you see on a listing is an *ask*, not a *sale*.

That does not make valuation arbitrary. Practitioners converge on the same handful of methods, and a credible price is usually the point where two or three of them agree.

## Method 1: Comparable sales

The closest thing the industry has to an objective benchmark is what similar names have actually sold for.

What counts as "similar" matters more than people expect. A good comparable matches on:

- **Length and structure** — six letters is a different asset from eighteen.
- **Word type** — a dictionary word, a compound (`freshdesk`), a coined brand (`zillow`), and a phrase behave differently.
- **TLD** — `.com` is its own category. A `.com` comp tells you almost nothing about a `.net` price, and vice versa.
- **Exact-match vs. partial-match** — partial matches trade at a steep discount.

Public sales databases and broker-reported figures exist, but they over-represent the large, unusual end of the market. The most useful comps are usually **private sales of genuinely similar names**, which is why brokers lean on their own transaction history.

## Method 2: Cost of development

Ask: *if a company wanted this name but could not buy it, what would they do?*

The honest answer is that they would either:

- **Buy a less suitable alternative** (a hyphenated version, a `.co`, a longer phrase) and pay some fraction of the price for a worse asset, or
- **Launch without it** and spend the difference acquiring customers.

This is why two-word `.com` names in mature categories price the way they do: the buyer is not paying for a string of characters, they are paying to skip three to five years of brand-building that the alternative requires.

## Method 3: Category authority

Some names are not brands — they are **the name of a thing**.

When a term *is* the category ("cloud", "legal", "guard"), the domain carries an authority position that a coined name cannot buy. The value shows up as:

- Direct type-in traffic from people looking for the category, not a brand.
- Instant comprehension in outbound email, PR, and sales conversations.
- Pricing power — a category-defining name supports premium positioning; a made-up one has to earn it.

Category names are scarce by construction. There is exactly one `ailegalguard.com`. That scarcity is the entire basis of the price.

## Method 4: Existing revenue and traffic

If the domain already earns money, it can be valued like an asset: **price ≈ annual profit × a multiple**, with adjustments for:

- **Traffic quality** — type-in and search traffic is durable; paid or referral traffic often is not.
- **Revenue concentration** — one affiliate partner is a risk; diversified income is not.
- **Transferability** — does the revenue survive the change of owner? For content and affiliate sites, often yes. For relationship-dependent income, often no.

Domains parked with pay-per-click earnings almost never justify their asking price on revenue alone. Revenue is a floor, not the whole story.

## Method 5: What the alternative costs

Sometimes the cleanest framing is subtractive:

> If we cannot have *this*, what do we spend — in money, time, and permanent brand damage — to get close?

That calculation includes advertising spend to compensate for lower recall, the cost of a worse name's rebrand later, and the customer confusion of a non-`.com` TLD. For a funded company entering a crowded category, this number is frequently larger than the asking price.

## What pushes a price up

- **`.com`** — still the default in email, on business cards, and in speech.
- **Short and pronounceable** — if you have to spell it over the phone, it costs you.
- **Exact category term** — the word people already use for the thing.
- **Clean history** — no trademark disputes, no spam past, no UDRP losses.
- **Meaningful to one obvious buyer** — a name with a natural owner has a natural ceiling *and* a natural bidder.

## What pushes a price down

- **Hyphens, numbers, and odd spelling** — each one is a leak in every conversation where the name is spoken aloud.
- **Length** — value falls off sharply past about twelve characters.
- **Trademark exposure in a live brand's class** — this can drive the value to zero regardless of how good the name sounds.
- **Ambiguity** — a name nobody can categorise has to explain itself first and sell second.

## The uncomfortable truth about asking prices

List prices are anchors. Some are anchored to a genuine market read; many are anchored to what the seller hopes a distracted buyer will pay.

The way through is to **triangulate**: find two or three real comparables, run the development-cost and alternative-cost math for your specific situation, and treat any single number — including the listed price — as a starting point for a conversation rather than a verdict.

If the name is a genuine one-of-one in your category, the question is not "is this worth it in the abstract." It is "what is the cost of *not* owning it, for us, over the next five years."
